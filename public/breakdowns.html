🟨 TIER 1 • EASIEST Direct Synonyms & Clear Match
CUTTING-EDGE
GROUP CONNECTION: ADVANCED, FOREMOST, MODERN, PROGRESSIVE all share the unifying bond: CUTTING-EDGE.
SOLVE STRATEGY: Verify all four words against CUTTING-EDGE before submitting to avoid 1-away traps with overlapping red herrings.
ADVANCED /ədˈvænst/ adjective
Far on or ahead in development, complexity, or progress; cutting-edge.
Origin & Note: From Old French avancier ('to move forward'), rooted in Latin abante ('from before').
"The lab introduced an advanced diagnostic tool that cut processing time in half."
FOREMOST /ˈfɔːrmoʊst/ adjective
Most prominent in rank, importance, or position; leading the field.
Origin & Note: From Old English formest, a double superlative meaning 'earliest or most in front.'
"She is widely regarded as the foremost authority on medieval linguistics."
MODERN /ˈmɒdərn/ adjective
Relating to the present or recent times; characterized by up-to-date techniques or ideas.
Origin & Note: From Late Latin modernus ('of today'), derived from Latin modo ('just now').
"Modern architecture often favors open floor plans and natural light."
PROGRESSIVE /prəˈɡrɛsɪv/ adjective
Happening or developing gradually; favoring new ideas, innovation, or reform.
Origin & Note: From Latin progressus ('a going forward'), from pro- ('forward') + gradi ('to walk').
"The company adopted a progressive remote-work policy ahead of its competitors."
🟩 TIER 2 • MEDIUM Shared Real-World Context
LOCATIONS FOR AN INSTITUTION
GROUP CONNECTION: CAMPUS, CENTER, COMPLEX, FACILITY all share the unifying bond: LOCATIONS FOR AN INSTITUTION.
SOLVE STRATEGY: Verify all four words against LOCATIONS FOR AN INSTITUTION before submitting to avoid 1-away traps with overlapping red herrings.
CAMPUS /ˈkæmpəs/ noun
The grounds and buildings of a university, college, hospital, or corporate institution.
Origin & Note: From Latin campus ('open field or plain'), first used for Princeton University's grounds in 1774.
"New students toured the brick walkways of the main campus before orientation."
CENTER /ˈsɛntər/ noun
A building, facility, or hub dedicated to a particular public, cultural, or research activity.
Origin & Note: From Greek kentron ('sharp point, stationary point of a compass') via Latin centrum.
"The community arts center hosts weekend pottery and painting workshops."
COMPLEX /ˈkɒmplɛks/ noun
A group of similar or interconnected buildings sharing a common site and purpose.
Origin & Note: From Latin complexus ('woven together'), from com- ('together') + plectere ('to braid').
"The new athletic complex includes an indoor track, pool, and tennis courts."
FACILITY /fəˈsɪlɪti/ noun
A place, amenity, or piece of equipment provided for a particular institutional purpose.
Origin & Note: From Latin facilis ('easy to do'), originally meaning ease before evolving to mean a helpful building.
"Engineers tested the prototype inside a climate-controlled research facility."
🟦 TIER 3 • HARD Trivia, Sets & Compound Links
STRATEGY BOARD GAMES
GROUP CONNECTION: CHESS, GO, OTHELLO, STRATEGO all share the unifying bond: STRATEGY BOARD GAMES.
SOLVE STRATEGY: Verify all four words against STRATEGY BOARD GAMES before submitting to avoid 1-away traps with overlapping red herrings.
CHESS /tʃɛs/ noun
A two-player abstract strategy board game played on an 8x8 checkered grid of 64 squares.
Origin & Note: From Old French esches, plural of eschec ('check'), ultimately from Persian shah ('king').
"Opening with the queen's pawn is a classic strategy in tournament chess."
GO /ɡoʊ/ noun
An ancient East Asian strategy board game where two players surround territory using black and white stones.
Origin & Note: Borrowed from Japanese igo ('surrounding board game'), derived from Middle Chinese weiqi.
"Despite simple placement rules, the game of Go has more legal board positions than atoms in the universe."
OTHELLO /oʊˈθɛloʊ/ noun
A classic two-player strategy board game played with reversible black-and-white discs (also called Reversi).
Origin & Note: Trademarked in 1971 by Goro Hasegawa, named after Shakespeare's play featuring dramatic reversals.
"One clever corner move in Othello can flip an entire row of your opponent's discs."
STRATEGO /strəˈtiːɡoʊ/ noun
A classic two-player strategy board game of hidden ranks, capture, and capturing the opponent's flag.
Origin & Note: Derived from Greek strategos ('military commander'), popularized as a board game in the mid-20th century.
"In Stratego, placing a bomb piece near your flag protects it from reckless scouts."
🟪 TIER 4 • TRICKY WORDPLAY Lateral Wordplay & Letter Shifts
BABY GEAR WITH FIRST LETTER CHANGED
GROUP CONNECTION: BATTLE, MARRIAGE, MOTTLE, TRIB all share the unifying bond: BABY GEAR WITH FIRST LETTER CHANGED.
SOLVE STRATEGY: Verify all four words against BABY GEAR WITH FIRST LETTER CHANGED before submitting to avoid 1-away traps with overlapping red herrings.
BATTLE /ˈbætəl/ noun
Wordplay: 'RATTLE' (baby toy) with its first letter changed from R to B.
Origin & Note: Standard word means combat (Latin battuere, 'to beat'), used here as a letter-swap decoy for a baby's rattle.
"Swap the 'B' back to 'R' and BATTLE becomes RATTLE—the noisy baby toy hidden in today's Purple tier."
MARRIAGE /ˈmærɪdʒ/ noun
Wordplay: 'CARRIAGE' (baby stroller/pram) with its first letter changed from C to M.
Origin & Note: Standard word means matrimony (Old French mariage), used here as a first-letter swap for a baby carriage.
"Change 'M' back to 'C' and MARRIAGE turns into CARRIAGE, completing the baby gear wordplay."
MOTTLE /ˈmɒtəl/ verb
Wordplay: 'BOTTLE' (baby milk bottle) with its first letter changed from B to M.
Origin & Note: Standard verb means to mark with spots or blotches; here it disguises a baby's bottle via a single-letter shift.
"Replacing the opening 'M' with 'B' transforms MOTTLE into BOTTLE."
TRIB /trɪb/ noun
Wordplay: 'CRIB' (baby bed) with its first letter changed from C to T.
Origin & Note: Short for tribune or printing term; here it serves as a one-letter disguise for a baby's crib.
"Swap 'T' for 'C' and TRIB reveals CRIB, finishing the four-item baby gear set."

Example: The "BAT" 5-Word Decoy Trap

5-WORD CONFLICT

The Dilemma: The board showed BAT, AIRPLANE, BIRD, BUTTERFLY, ANGEL. All five have wings! Guessing right away would waste an attempt because one belongs to a different group.

The Solution: BAT actually belonged to playing card suits alongside CLUB, DIAMOND, and HEART (with baseball wordplay). Once you see the suits, the four true winged items become obvious!

Example: Words That End in "BOARD"

BLUE TIER PATTERN

The Pattern: Words that don't mean the same thing at all (KEY, DASH, SNOW, SURF) were secretly linked because each one can end with the word "BOARD".

The Takeaway: When words seem completely unrelated, try adding a common word like "BOARD", "TIME", or "BALL" before or after them.
